# Silence in Heaven: Route B

A second alternate route. Val doesn't recover after April dies in the Settlement. She goes back with one purpose, to take everything back, and Heaven goes the way of episode 26 before the ending goes the way of *The End of Evangelion*: no reset and no new world.

**Where it branches:** Chapters 1–14 are unchanged, and so is Chapter 15 up to "Val sat in the field until morning." in Arc 66. Route B then runs through Chapters 16–19 in full, and Chapter 19's coda replaces the Epilogue. Chapters 16–17 mostly follow the main version, with Val's changes listed in the notes at the end. Chapters 18–19 are new.

## Chapter 15 (Route B): The Settlement

*Chapter 15 runs exactly as in the main version until Arc 66, "Val sat in the field until morning." From there:*

### Arc 66: Am I April? (Route B)

Val sat in the field until morning.

She put her head down on her knees. Not on a table; there was no table out here. On her knees, in the frost, the way she had put it on a school desk for seven years so nobody could reach her. She stayed like that under the buzzing lamps and felt the cold come up out of the ground into her, and let it.

There was nothing left unsaid. That was the worst part. April had asked everything she wanted to ask, and Val had answered all of it, and it hadn't kept her. Answering didn't keep anyone. Tom had pushed rice balls across a table every morning. Sae had written *Your turn* and taken a seat. Kuze had remembered her name. Abriel had gone up like snow falling in reverse. Every one of them had picked the seat, and every one of them was gone, and here was the last of them, lying on the earth in a fine glittering layer, over the rows she had planted.

*Everybody who stands next to you dies.* Gage had said it quietly, in a hangar, a long time ago. He hadn't been shouting. That was what made it true.

Somewhere near dawn, something in her went flat and still and very clear, the way the plain had been clear the morning after the Third War: nothing left on it but ice.

Heaven could make a girl out of wanting. It had done it once. It had made her out of frost and out of how badly Val wanted her back, and she had sat at a kitchen table for forty-one days and asked about the sky.

If it could do that, it could give her back. It could give all of them back. It could take all of it back: the plant, the port, the white room, the plain, the field. Every bit of it. All the way back to a principal's office, and a pencil, and a man in a rumpled coat kneeling on the carpet. She had been the reason the door opened once. She could be the reason again.

It was the first plan she had ever had that was only hers. It felt like the first warm thing in a long time.

That should have frightened her. It didn't.

When the sun came up gray over the hills, she stood, brushed the frost off her knees, and walked down through the waking town to the clinic, where Seus was splitting firewood badly in the yard.

He saw her coming and stopped. He looked at her face, and something in his own face closed, the way a door closes on a draft. He looked past her, up the hill, toward the fields, where there was only one figure walking and there should have been two.

"I'm going back," she told him.

He put down the axe. He didn't ask where. He didn't ask why. Maybe he already knew. Maybe he didn't want to.

"Yeah," he said. "Me too."

Behind him, under the tarp in the snow, the crate he said wasn't his was very quiet, the way something is quiet when it's listening.

### Arc 67: Return

Haruto raised WAKE on the settlement's old radio.

It took him most of a day. The radio lived in the generator shed, a military set from before 2044 that he had rebuilt twice out of parts from other radios, and it took Seus's memory of the WAKE frequencies and a great deal of swearing from both of them before anything came out of the speaker but static. When something finally did, it was a woman's voice, tired and clipped: a comms officer on a night shift, somewhere far away, asking who was on this band and how they'd gotten the code.

Seus took the microphone out of Haruto's hand.

"This is Theseus Moriah, off the Vigil," he said. "I'm in a settlement called Kasumi, in the hills, about a hundred and forty kilometers southwest of the ruins. I've got Kane."

There was silence on the line. Not static: silence, the open kind, of someone holding a microphone and not saying anything.

Then a different voice came on.

"Say that again."

Val, sitting on a crate in the corner of the shed with her hands between her knees, knew the voice. So did Seus. He looked at her across the shed, and something complicated went across his face.

"Kane," he said into the microphone. "Val. She's alive. She's sitting right here."

The silence on the line went on for a long time. Long enough that Haruto reached over to check the dials.

"Two days," Alex said, and the line clicked off.

The Vigil came for them two days later.

It came over the hills at dusk, enormous and gray and scarred, lower than it should have been, with one side of it blackened and the flight surfaces on that side a raw new pink where they had been grafted again. It was too big to land anywhere near Kasumi. It hung over the hills above the valley with the steam rising around it, blotting out half the sky, and every person in the settlement came out of their houses and stood in the streets and looked up at it with their mouths open. Then a transport detached from its belly and came down, rotors thumping, and settled in the square by the creek.

The core came first. Seus carried it himself, out from behind the clinic, out from under the tarp, in his arms, wrapped in blankets, the way he had carried it down from orbit. He didn't let anyone help him. He walked it across the square and up the transport's ramp and set it down inside, and came back out, and didn't look at anyone.

Val went to the fields first.

She went down the hill alone, in the last of the light, to the row under the heat lamps, and crouched at the end of it. The seedling was still there. It had two leaves now. She looked at it for a while. Then she went back up the hill and found Nanami at the door of the house, with Hinata on her hip.

"The seedling," Val said. "At the end of the row. April's."

"Every day," said Nanami, before she could ask. "I'll water it every day."

At the transport, Akari hugged Val and told her that if she died, Akari would personally dig her up and kill her again.

"I mean it," Akari said into her shoulder. "I'll find you. I'll find wherever they put you and I'll dig you up with a spoon."

"Okay," said Val.

Haruto gave her a camera. It was his good one, the one in the padded box.

"It's got the sky on it," he said, when she tried to give it back. "The school roof. The cherry trees. In case you need to show anyone." He pushed it back into her hands and folded her fingers around it. "I've got four more. Take it. You're still my subject. I want footage."

Kōta asked if he could come. Akari said no before he had finished the sentence.

Nanami came last. She stood in front of Val in the square, in the noise of the rotors, with the baby on her hip and the steam blowing around them, and she smiled the smile she had smiled every morning for a year in the doorway of Class 2-A, and every morning for two months over a bowl of rice.

"Good morning, Val," she said, even though it was evening.

Val looked at her.

She didn't say anything.

She could feel it there, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue: two words, small, easy, the ones she had said back every morning for weeks in a kitchen that smelled of miso. They wouldn't come. There was a field above the town with frost on it, and the words had gone into it with everything else.

Nanami kept smiling. It was a good smile. It had had a lot of practice at not being answered. She reached out and straightened the collar of Val's parka, the way she straightened the laminated schedules on the school wall, and stepped back, and didn't cry, and Val got on the transport without looking back at her.

Akari punched Seus in the shoulder, hard.

"Come back," she said.

"Obviously," said Seus. He didn't look at her. Then he did.

On the ship, Alex met her in the hangar bay.

The Vigil's bay was half dark. Most of the refugees from Ararat were on the ground now, scattered through the settlements along the southern coast; the ship carried only the crew who had volunteered to stay, and it felt empty, enormous, echoing. Half the lights were out. There were scorch marks across the deck where the crystal storm had come through the open doors. At the far end, bolted into the ship's spine with cables as thick as trees, Unit 04 hung in its cradle with its chest glowing faintly through the plating, and Val felt it the moment she stepped off the transport, the way you feel your own pulse in a cut.

Alex stood in the middle of the deck. He was thinner. His face had new lines in it, and the old ones were deeper, and he looked at her for a long moment without saying anything, the way you look at something you had stopped letting yourself picture.

Then, without a word, he came across the deck, and lifted a new Halo, and fastened it around her throat himself, one-handed. It clicked shut, cold against her skin.

"Procedure," he said, and his voice cracked on it.

He stepped back. He turned around and walked away across the bay, fast, and didn't look back, and the hatch closed behind him.

Maggie, leaning against Lizzie's leg with her arms folded, hummed something old and winked at her.

"Told him," she said. "For two months, I told him. *She's not dead, Alex. I'd know.*" She pushed off from Lizzie's leg and came over and looked Val up and down, and her eyes, which were much older than her face, went very bright. "He didn't listen. He never listens. Welcome back, kiddo."

That night, in the wrecked mess hall, Alex told them what was coming.

There were twenty of them around the one long table that wasn't broken: the bridge crew, the hangar chief, a handful of engineers, Maggie with her boots up, Seus at the far end with his arms folded. Val sat in the middle with a tray in front of her she hadn't touched. Alex stood at the head of the table with his one hand flat on it.

"Total Assimilation," he said. "It's what the people above Umbral have been building toward since before any of us were born."

He looked around the table.

"Every one of you has seen a seal. Every exo raises one. Every Nephil has one; it's the thing we have to break before we can touch them. What nobody tells you is that you've got one too." He touched his own chest with two fingers. "Everybody does. It's what keeps you you. It's what keeps you in. It's in the files we brought out of the archive, in their own words." He let that sit. "You all know what happens when a pilot's seal breaks in the capsule."

"They go soft," said the hangar chief.

"They want to do that to everyone. All at once. Every seal on Earth stripped, every mind running together into one. No more loneliness. No more pain. No more separate people, so nobody left to be on the other side of a war." His mouth twisted. "They call it salvation. They mean it. They call the ceremony the Rite, and it starts tomorrow night."

Nobody said anything.

"How do you know?" said the hangar chief finally.

"Because they've been moving everything they have into the ruins of headquarters for a week. Every white unit in the sky. Every troop transport. And because the scar's been getting brighter every night since the Third War, and last night it started to hum." Alex looked down at the table. "And because it's in their book. Chapter and verse. They've been reading it like a schedule for fifty years, and they're right on time."

"The door's shut," said someone down the table. "Abriel shut it."

"Scarred shut. Something has to open it again. Their book says it opens tomorrow night. It never says how. It doesn't have to." He was quiet for a second. "They'll have arranged someone."

"What about Patrick?" Val asked.

Alex didn't answer right away.

"Nobody's heard from him in days," he said.

Val heard every word of it and kept one sentence. *Their book says it opens tomorrow night.* A door into the place that gave you what you wanted most. She sat in the middle of the table with her tray untouched and felt something in her chest go still and sure, the way it had in the field. She didn't say anything. Nobody asked her what she wanted. Nobody ever had, except a girl made of frost.

Across the table, Seus touched the edge of his eyepatch, the way he did when it talked to him, and said nothing.

In the morning, there was tamagoyaki on her tray.

It was torn down the middle and burnt along one edge, the way it had been in Ararat after Tom was gone. She looked at it for a long time. She didn't eat it. She left it on the tray, torn side up. On her way out of the mess, she passed the night cook coming off shift, who was complaining to someone that the commander had been in the kitchen at four in the morning again, making a mess of the good pan, which was strange, because the commander never ate anything.

Val heard it. She didn't think about it. She had other things to think about.

The next night, at midnight, the scar over the ruins of Hakobune began to strain.

It started at the middle. The long pale line from the hills to the top of the world, which had hung healed over the ruins since the Third War, began to glow, and then to burn, and then to bulge, the way a seam bulges when something pushes against it from inside. It didn't split. Not yet. It hummed, the sound of every bell in the world being rung very softly at once, and the light that leaked along it lay across the frozen world like the light under a door.

Pilotless units circled it, white and faceless, hundreds of them, like moths around a lamp. The Conclave's troops held the ruins of headquarters below, in black armor, around the shaft, with weapons that had never been meant for the Nephilim. They were waiting for something.

The Vigil flew straight into it.

### Arc 68: Father and Son

Seus launched first.

His patched-together unit stood in the Vigil's bay, built around the core he had carried down from orbit. WAKE had found the wreck on the plain on their way to Kasumi and dragged it home, and the engineers had spent two days and nights putting the core back into it, and it was uglier than ever: three hollows' worth of white armor, a second-generation unit's long gray arms, a chest plate welded shut over a split that still showed, and a fresh coat of primer over all of it, the color of nothing. Its name was still blank on its shoulder.

He climbed into the capsule. The walls lit. The link opened, rough and grinding, like a door in an old house.

As the bay doors opened onto the light, it spoke to him the way it always did, the way it had from under a tarp behind a clinic in the snow.

"Pilot. Ready?"

For the first time, he answered.

"Your name's Navis," he told it. "Don't make it weird."

There was a pause in the link. A long one. He felt it, the way you feel someone on the other end of a phone take a breath.

"Was that good?" the unit asked, very quietly, in a voice that wasn't the old Navis's and was anyway.

Seus closed his eye. It wasn't him. He knew that. He'd said so to a lantern. He named it anyway.

"Yeah," he said. "That was good."

He dropped into the light.

Far below, at the edge of the shaft above the Reliquary, Patrick Moriah stood alone with his hands behind his back, watching the scar.

He stood exactly where he had stood every day since the Almost, on a ledge of broken floor at the lip of the hole in the middle of the ruins, in his coat, with the glow from the bottom of the shaft lighting his face from underneath and the light of the scar lighting it from above. The Conclave's voices were in his ear. They had been in his ear all night, calm, pleased, reading to each other from their book. He answered them when they asked him something. "Yes," he said. "Yes. On schedule. Yes."

Then he took something out of his coat.

It was a folder, gray, gone soft at the corners, sealed with tape that had yellowed years ago and been cut once, carefully, and taped shut again. Sam had given it to him in a hangar at night, the year before the end, coughing into his sleeve. *Hold this,* Sam had said. *When the time is right, you'll know.*

*Know what?*

*You'll know.*

That was all. That was the whole instruction. Patrick had opened the folder once, the night after the funeral, alone in his office, and read it through, every page, in Sam's cramped capitals: what was buried in 04's core and how to reach it, what the Root was, what the swords were, what it would take to open the door and what to send up the stair once it was open. It told him everything to do. It never told him why. He had taped it shut again and carried it in the inside pocket of every coat he had owned for fourteen years. He had held it through the principal's office and seven years of a gray car at a school gate. He had held it through a port, and through a son walking eleven days across the ice to stand in front of his desk. He had held it, the way he held everything, on the hope that one day it would turn out to have been worth it.

"It'll be worth it," Patrick said, to no one. "It has to be."

He put the folder back in his coat and went down the service ladder into the shaft, slowly, the way a man his age climbs down a ladder.

Far down, where the glow came from, the Root lay curled in the frost like something not yet born, pale as the inside of a shell. Two swords stood in it, side by side, where Umbral had set them back after the Third War. One burned with a cold fire that flickered like a flame in wind, always about to go out. The other burned perfectly steady, the way it had since a boy with pale hair shut a door with it and died.

Just above it, hangar zero hung out over the drop on its steel arms, and in its cradle, Unit 12 waited, rebuilt from its own wreck.

Patrick's people had found it on the frozen plain the week after the Third War, black and broken, with crystal still growing down one arm and a mask half shattered over its face, and they had brought it back and rebuilt it in hangar zero without asking him why, because nobody in Patrick's Umbral asked him anything. The crystal was gone now. The mask was gone. It was black glass again, tall and narrow, with its two capsules side by side along its spine.

He opened the capsule. He looked for a moment at the two seats.

He sat in the seat Abriel had died in.

The folder had told him what it took. Three kinds of flesh in one body, and a sword that was willing. Unit 12 had been grown from the Root; it was the saint. He was the man. That was two.

He waited for the third.

High above him, Seus was losing.

There were too many white units. There were always too many. They came at Navis from every side, out of the light, in silence, with nobody inside them to be afraid, and they tore at the patched armor with their long white hands. Every wound went straight into Seus's own body. A plate came off Navis's shoulder and his shoulder burned. A white hand closed on Navis's leg and his leg went numb. He fought them off, and more came, and behind him the Vigil came on through the light, slow and enormous, taking hits all along its length, and the white units began to turn toward it.

The Vigil needed a way through to the shaft. He was supposed to make it one. He couldn't. Not like this.

And under his eyepatch, the thing behind it was awake.

It had been awake since the scar started to burn. It was kicking. It was laughing. It was saying the thing it always said, the thing it had said for six years in the dark behind his eye, like a kid kicking the back of a seat, except louder now, so much louder, filling his whole head.

*Let me out. Let me out. Let me out and we can win.*

He thought about Akari tying the bandage too tight on purpose.

He thought about the Vigil, and Val on its bridge, and Kasumi under the light, a woman with a baby looking up.

"Akari's going to kill me," Seus said.

So he reached up and tore the patch off his eye.

What was behind it woke up.

Frost poured out of him. It poured out of his eye like light out of a lamp, through the link, into Navis, and the patched chassis around him cracked and grew. The welds split. The mismatched plates groaned and shifted and fused. Crystal ran down Navis's arms, white and branching, the way it had run down Unit 12's arms on the day the sky turned to glass. Its fingers lengthened. And over its blank primer-colored face, something began to form, pale and smooth and beautiful, like a mask carved out of ice.

For a moment Seus was almost one of the giants he used to fight. He felt it. He felt what they had felt, falling over the river, over the harbor, over the plant: a terrible hunger to go home, and a terrible strength, and no fear at all.

He tore through the white units like paper.

They came apart in his hands. They came apart when he looked at them. He went through them in a long white line toward the shaft, opening a road for the Vigil through the swarm, laughing, or the thing in his eye was laughing, he couldn't tell the difference anymore.

"Seus!" Maggie screamed over the channel. "Put it back! Kid, put it back, that's what he—"

Unit 12 rose out of the shaft.

It came up out of the ruins of headquarters black and silent, and the white units parted for it.

He had known Seus would tear off the patch. He had counted on it. He had known it from the moment he saw it, in the doorway of his office in the ruins, the year after the Almost, when a boy who should have been dead came in out of eleven days of ice and stood in front of his desk with a black patch over one eye. He had known what was behind it. Abriel was gone. Only one Nephil was left in the world, and it was in his son's eye.

On the open channel, Seus heard breathing.

He knew that breathing. He had grown up at the other branch, across the sea, with his mother, and his father had called on his birthday most years. Not every year. Most. And every time, before he said anything, there had been this: a long slow breath on the line, in and out, like a man standing at the edge of cold water deciding whether to go in. Seus had learned to wait through it. He had learned to count it. Sometimes it went on so long that his mother took the phone away.

Navis turned in the light, crystal-armed, half masked, and faced the black unit rising toward it.

"Dad?" said Seus.

The breathing went on. In, and out.

"Dad. I know it's you." His voice came out wrong, too young. "Say something."

In, and out.

"Say something. Just once."

In Unit 12's capsule, in the seat where Abriel had died, Patrick Moriah sat with his hands on the controls. His jaw was working. His eyes were open. He was looking at the half-masked thing in front of him, which had his son inside it, and his mouth opened, once, as if something were going to come out of it.

Nothing did.

Unit 12 reached out and took Navis.

It didn't strike it. It simply opened, the way a mouth opens, the black glass of its chest unfolding in long petals, and closed around Navis and the boy inside. The frost pouring out of Seus's eye poured into it instead, into the black glass, into the Root-grown flesh and the man in its seat, and the light from Seus's eye went out of the sky.

Unit 12 went back down into the shaft with what it had taken, fast, past the broken levels and the scorched frame of the old drive wing, down into the blue, until it hung above the Root.

At the bottom of the shaft, the swords tore loose from the Root.

They came up out of the frost end over end, like two sparks going up a chimney, the way the first of them had once come up the same shaft for Navis, to stop it. They stopped a hand's width from Unit 12's glass and hung there, side by side, the flickering one and the steady one, and their light lay across the black glass and across the man inside it.

Patrick felt them look at him. There was no other word for it.

He didn't know what they saw. Fourteen years of a folder in his coat. Twenty years of holding. And under all of it, older and plainer than any plan, the thing he had wanted since a hangar at three in the morning: for it to turn out to have been worth something.

The swords turned, and went into Unit 12, the way keys go into a lock they were cut for.

Inside it, the steady one began, very slightly, to flicker.

And the door opened.

It opened right above Unit 12, deep in the shaft, where there was nothing to open, and it kept opening: straight up the shaft and out of the ruins and along the old scar to the top of the sky. The scar split from the middle outward, the way a seam splits when something pushes against it from inside, and widened, past where it had been in the Almost, past where it had been when Unit 12 touched it the first time, until it filled the horizon, a wall of light from one edge of the world to the other. And at the bottom of it, deep in the shaft where it had begun, just above Unit 12, the light ran down into the heart of it, into the place where everything was going to go. It opened quietly. It opened the way a door opens in a house where someone is sleeping.

Total Assimilation poured out of it, and up toward it.

In Patrick's ear, the Conclave's voices had stopped reading. They were saying his name now, in the tone of men who had just understood that something was wrong.

"Commander Moriah. The door is open. You are to hold it from the shaft. Going through is not on the schedule. You are not authorized to—"

"I held," Patrick said. "The time is right."

He cut them off.

Patrick never said a word to his son. He never had.

Then Unit 12 went into the light at the bottom of the door, carrying what it needed, with the two swords burning inside it like lamps carried into a dark house. Behind it, the way down stayed open.

### Arc 69: The Trigger

From the bridge of the Vigil, Val watched it happen.

She stood at the long window with her hands flat on the glass, with the light of the scar pouring over everything, and watched a black shape rise out of the ruins and open like a flower and close around Navis. She heard Seus on the channel. She heard him say *Dad.* She heard the breathing. She heard it stop.

Then the door opened, and filled the sky, and the black shape went back down into it.

On the monitors along the bridge, the feeds came in: settlements, coastal towns, the last relay stations still working. Across the frozen world, people were looking up. In streets and fields and on rooftops, they stopped what they were doing and lifted their faces to the light, and a look came over them, peaceful, almost relieved, like someone setting down a heavy bag at the end of a long walk. And then they began to come apart. Slowly, gently, into frost and light, from the edges in, the way frost comes off a window when the sun hits it: every seal on Earth going soft at once. They rose. Thousands of them. Millions. Rising off the earth like snow falling in reverse, toward the door.

Val knew that. She had seen it, in a capsule, in her own arms. It was how Abriel had gone.

Somewhere, in a valley full of steam, a woman with a baby on her hip was looking up.

The Vigil was coming apart.

The road Seus had opened through the swarm was closing behind them. The white units had found the ship. They were all over it, on its back, on its flanks, tearing at the grafted pink flight surfaces with their long hands, and the whole ship shuddered under her feet with every hit. Alarms were going off all over the bridge, one on top of another, until they stopped meaning anything. Somebody was shouting damage reports. Below the bridge window, in the hangar bay, Unit 04 hung in its cradle in the ship's spine, and the cables that fed the ship from its core were tearing loose, one by one, and whipping across the bay floor, throwing sparks.

Alex knew they wouldn't make it all the way down. Val could see it in the way he was standing.

He came and stood beside her at the window.

"He went down," Alex said. "Patrick. He took Seus with him."

Val didn't say anything. She couldn't.

Then Alex held out his hand.

The Halo's trigger sat in his palm. The small black switch. She had watched it in his hand on the bridge as she walked across the bay toward a white unit, with his thumb on it. She had felt it every day in Ararat, on every mission, a millimeter from her life. He had held it for a year and never pressed it. He was holding it out to her.

"You decide what's important for you," he said.

It was the answer to her question, years late. She had screamed it in a capsule in a port, alone, with her comms off, so nobody would hear. *Who the fuck decides what's important for me?* Somebody had heard. Alex read every log. He had found it in the capsule's audio weeks later, alone, at four in the morning, and never said a word about it. *You do.* You always should have.

Val took it.

It was lighter than she'd expected. She closed her hand around it and looked down through the bridge window, at the bay, at Unit 04 hanging in its cradle with its chest glowing through the plating. She had felt it since the transport. She had felt it every minute since she came aboard, the way you feel your own pulse. And under the cradle, under the suppression, under whatever the Morningstar and a war and a door had done to it, under the place where the rage was sleeping, there was something else. Something small. She didn't know what it was. She only knew it was there, the way you know someone is in a dark room before they speak.

"April," she said. And then, because it was the truth: "All of them."

Alex looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face, he didn't argue with it. Maybe there wasn't time. Maybe he understood it too well.

"Then go," he said.

He put his hand flat on the command console and pulled the cradle release.

Down in the bay, the Enoch cradle around 04's core opened like a hand letting go. The last cables tore loose all at once with a sound like a building coming down. And every light on the Vigil went out.

The consoles died. The alarms died. The deep hum that had been coming up through the floor since Ararat, the sound of the ship breathing, stopped. In the sudden silence, the emergency lights came on, dim and red, and the whole bridge was the color of the inside of a closed eye. The ship lurched. Its nose dipped. Without the core, it had no power to fly, only its long grown wings, and it began to fall forward and down, slowly, in a long tilting glide, toward the ruins.

"We'll never make it to the shaft like this," Alex said. "So I'm going to do something stupid."

He smiled. And for a moment he was twenty-three again, in an apartment kitchen, burning omurice one-handed and laughing at himself while a girl at the table watched.

"Go," he said. "The bay. Go."

She turned to go. She got as far as the hatch.

"Val. Wait."

She stopped.

When she looked back, he was standing at the dead console in the red light, with his one hand on it, and his face was doing something she had never seen it do. It was coming apart. Not like the people under the door, gently. All at once.

"There's no time," he said, "so just listen. Seus. I got him out. Not you, not Umbral, not luck. Me and Aoi. After the port, his vitals spiked, once, I saw it. We took him. ### Arc 71: Rooms

### Arc 72: The Fourth War

At the center of Heaven was a stair, and at the top of the stair was a throne.

She had taken the test that day, instead of the day off she was owed, because she was a little afraid of the long afternoon at home. She had been planning to be better at it later.

And she saw her father.

Nobody spoke. He didn't turn to her. He didn't know she was there. She only saw.

She saw a boy of seventeen come around a corner and see his house. She saw him climb into a dead man's machine because he had to hit something or die. She saw the medals, and the talk shows, and the canteen where the most famous pilot in the world ate alone. She saw him meet a woman with short hair and a pencil, and be happy, for four years, more than he knew how to be. She saw the phone call. She saw him sit on the floor against a door all night. She saw a two-year-old asleep in a crib, and a man look at her, and be so afraid of loving her and losing her the way he had lost everyone that he walked out of the room.

She saw the doorway.

She saw it from his side, this time. A summer evening, the cicadas going, a pencil in his hand, and a girl of six behind an apron, looking at the floor. And she saw what he saw: not a girl choosing not to answer. A girl of six. Shy. Looking at the floor the way six-year-olds do. And she saw him decide, standing there, that the floor was an answer, and that the answer was no. Because he was the one who was afraid. Because it was easier to be refused than to stay and find out.

It hadn't been her silence. It had been what he did with it.

She saw him across the two-lane street, at the school gate, behind a parked car, every month. She saw the postcards, every month, until a month before he died. She saw the facility, and the twenty-six, and the one in the tank he didn't see. She saw him lie, afterward, to himself. She saw him build a war around her because grief needed something big enough to throw itself at. She saw him change his mind, sick, with a year left. She saw him sitting in Pip's dark capsule, night after night, building something small and curious beside the war, and putting his hand on Pip's leg, as high as he could reach, and saying *please.*

He had been just as broken as she was.

All of them had. Every one. Some of them more than she was. Some of them so much more.

And there was one place she couldn't see into. Far out, past the edge of the warm, on the ice at the lip of the shaft, there was a single seal still whole. Small. Stubborn. Out of reach. Someone who had never come in. She couldn't see what was inside it. She didn't know what he was thinking. She would have to ask.

She looked at all of them for a long time.

Last of all, she saw herself.

She saw a girl in the back row of a classroom with her head on her arms. A girl at a school gate getting into a gray car without a word. A girl in a hangar saying *Sorry, knowing's not my thing* to a boy who had come a long way to meet her. A girl in a port with her hands down. She saw herself from outside, the way all of them had seen her, and she wasn't the center of any of it. She wasn't the reason for any of it. She wasn't the worst thing in any of their lives. She was one more frightened person in a world full of them, doing her best and getting it wrong, the same as everybody.

It didn't fix anything. She understood all of them now, all the way down, and none of them were any less dead, and nothing she had done was any less done.

But it wasn't supposed to fix anything.

It was only true.

## Chapter 19 (Route B): Morning, Anyway

> *While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.*

> — Genesis 8:22

### Arc 80: The Blank Page

The ring didn't last. She hadn't thought it would.

One by one they went back into the warm, not because anything made them, but because that was where they were. Asleep in the light, every one of them, with the Rite still pulling, and the ring had only been the shape of them, standing up for a moment to say a word. Nanami went last. She lifted Hinata's small hand and waved it, and then there was only white ground, and Val standing on it.

The Rite was still there. She could feel it all around her, the whole weight of it: every mind on Earth with its seal gone, running together into one bright sea. It was offering her the same thing it had offered her at the desk, in all their voices at once, warm and close and gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

*Stay. Stay with us. You'll never be alone again. No more loneliness. No more pain. No more separate people. Just us. Forever.*

She knew where it went now. She had been there. She had sat in it for longer than she could measure, and it didn't hurt, and nobody in it was anybody, and there was a word in it with nowhere to go.

She said no.

Not to protect herself, this time. Not because closeness was dangerous, or because wanting things killed people, or because it was safer to be nobody. She had tried being nobody. She had done it all the way down.

She said no because she had seen every one of them for exactly who they were, all the way down, as frightened as she was, and it still wasn't the same as being with them. Because Sae had sat a meter away on the hangar steps, and it had mattered that it was a meter, and that it was Sae. Because Tom had pushed a rice ball across a table, and it had mattered that there was a table, and someone on the other side of it. Because Nanami had said good morning, every day, to someone who never said it back, and it had mattered, it had mattered more than anything, that Val was someone else. Someone who could say it back.

"You can't say good morning to someone," Val said, to the light, to all of them, "if they're you."

Everything here was kept. Nothing here had ever begun.

She could have rewritten it. She knew that, standing there on the white ground with the trigger in her hand. She could feel how it would go, the way you feel the shape of a word before you say it.

What came into her free hand was a book.

Heaven made whatever people believed in. People believed in stories. They believed a world could be written down. So it gave her one to write in: a plain book with a soft cover, like a school notebook, and every page in it was blank.

She sat down on the white ground and opened it on her knee. She took her mother's pencil out of her pocket and held it over the first page.

She thought of the other book, open on a long dark table, every page of it full, every margin scheduled in pencil all the way to the end. She thought of a gray folder in a man's coat, with every step in it and no reasons. Of a boy on the Moon doing what a girl had asked him, over and over, for a face he couldn't even remember. Of her father in a hangar at night, building a war around her, and then building something small beside it, and hoping.

Everyone who had ever loved her had written her into something.

She could write one line. One small, kind line, and let the rest grow from it. It would still be her line. Every person in that light would wake up inside a sentence she had chosen for them, and never know it was there, the way she had lived seven years inside a plan she never knew was there.

The pencil stayed where it was, a few millimeters above the page.

It didn't move.

She closed the book.

"I'm not going to write you," she said. She said it to the light, to all of them: to Nanami counting heads in a valley full of steam, and Akari with her hand on Kōta's shoulder, and Hinata going bright at the edges, and Alex at a kitchen table watching someone eat his cooking, and every one of the millions she would never know. "I'm not going to fix it. I'm not going to start it over. It's yours. If you want to come back, come back. As you. I'll be out there." She stopped. It sounded too small. It was all she had. "I won't make you."

Heaven gave you what you wanted most. That had always been the trouble with it.

So it gave them that.

The Rite stopped pulling. Not all at once. It slowed, the way a tide slows at the turn, and the light that had been pouring up through the door hung still, and then, very slowly, began to settle. Out past the rooms, the bright sea that had been everyone stopped running together. It didn't come apart. It only stopped. Each of them was still in it, warm and soft and without edges, and each of them could stay there forever, and nobody would ever ask them anything again.

Or they could get up.

That was all she gave them. Not a world. A door they could open from their side, if they wanted to, one at a time.

But a door that big couldn't be closed from the outside. It had to be held shut from in here.

She felt it as soon as she had let go. The door was still open behind everything. Shut from out there, it would only be a door with Heaven leaning on it, the way Heaven had leaned on Hakobune for fifty years: the Falls, the frost, the wanting coming through every crack. And down in the light behind them, inside Unit 12 where it had come to rest, the two swords were still burning, one steady and one flickering. As long as they were anywhere in the world, somebody would find a want big enough to turn them.

Someone had to stay on the inside and hold it shut. Someone had to keep the keys, and the rage, and the exos and the giants, and everything else that came from here and could never go back out.

She stood up.

She left the book where it was, closed, on the white ground, with the pencil lying across its cover. It was the last thing she had from before. It could stay before.

Then she climbed the stair one more time.

The Beast was still kneeling on the top step in front of the empty chair, where she had left it, with its chest glowing faintly through the plating. She put her hand flat on it, over the core. It was warm.

"Val?" said the small thing inside. "Are you back?"

"I'm going," Val said. "You can't come. You came from here. Everything that came from here has to stay." She didn't know how she knew that. She knew it the way you know a door is locked before you try the handle. "But I'll answer. When you ask. Every time."

The small thing thought about it, very seriously.

"Later," it said.

"Later," Val agreed.

She reached up and unclasped the Halo from her throat, and set it on the chair, with its trigger beside it. Nobody was using the chair. Nobody had to hold the Halo anymore. Not even her.

Then she went down the stair by herself, with nobody's hand in hers, and walked out across the white toward the edge of the light.

She looked back once.

There were three of them at the door. She was too far away to see their faces, and they didn't call to her, and she didn't call to them. She only saw them. A man in a coat, standing a little apart, the way he always stood. A woman with short hair, who bent and picked something up off the white ground, a book, a pencil, and turned the pencil over in her fingers and kept it. And a man with a crooked grin she couldn't see from here and didn't need to, who lifted one arm, the whole arm, the way a man waves at a school gate when he's arrived embarrassingly early.

Behind them, Unit 12 knelt in the light with two swords burning inside it, one steady and one flickering, and the Beast knelt at the top of the stair. Everything that had come from here. All of it, staying.

Then the three of them closed the door from the inside, and the light closed over all of them, and there was nothing to wave at.

* * *

She woke up cold.

She was lying on her back on the ice at the lip of the shaft, in the ruins of headquarters, and the sky above her was dark.

It took her a long time to understand what was wrong with it. Then she did. There was no seam. For the first time since the Almost, there was no long bright wound from the hills to the top of the world. There was only sky, low and gray-black and ordinary, and behind it, very faint, the dim white coin of the sun. The Moon was still cracked. Nothing up there was looking down.

She sat up.

The light had come down while she was gone. It lay over everything now, as far as she could see, a fine glittering frost that didn't melt, faintly lit from inside, the way snow looks with a lamp buried under it. Over the ruins. Over the plain. Over the hills, all the way to where Fuji burned on the horizon, small and orange. Everybody on Earth was under it, she understood. Every one of them. Warm and soft and without edges, lying under the frost of the world like seeds under a field.

Waiting. Or not waiting. That was up to them now.

The districts that had hung upside down over the city for seven years had come down while she was gone. Somebody had set them down, gently, still upside down, in the frost, the way you put toys away when you don't know which way up they go. A school sat on its roof with its playground in the air. A street of houses lay with its gardens on top, the trees hanging down into the gray.

Across the shaft, the Vigil lay broken over the ruins where it had come down, black now, not burning anymore, with the frost already growing over it like moss. She didn't look at it for long.

There were no exos anywhere. No white units. No Beast. Nothing at the bottom of the shaft but cold.

Lizzie was gone too. Where she had gone down at the edge of the shaft, there was only a long scrape in the frost, and in the middle of it the shape of a capsule seat, pressed into the ice, empty. A line of small boot prints went away from it across the frost, south, toward the hills. They didn't come back.

Val looked at them for a long time. Nobody had asked Maggie to go, she thought. Nobody had asked her to stay.

She touched her throat. It was bare. It felt colder than the rest of her.

Seus was lying a few meters away, on his side, in the frost where Navis had been.

The door had taken Navis with everything else that came from Heaven. He had been sitting inside it when the door shut, and now there was nothing around him but a dent in the frost the shape of a seat. His flight suit was torn and his face was gray with frost, and the patch was gone from his eye, and the eye was just an eye. He was breathing. She crawled over to him across the ice, and checked, twice, to be sure, and then she sat down beside him. Not close. A meter away. Knees up.

She waited.

He woke the way people wake up in hospitals: all at once, and then slowly. He looked at the sky. He looked at the frost, and the upside-down school, and the empty orange unit by the shaft. He looked at her.

"Where is everyone?"

"In there." She nodded at the frost. At everything. "All of them. They can come back. If they want to."

"If they want to."

"Yes."

He was quiet. "Where's my dad?"

"He stayed. Him and my parents. Somebody had to hold the door shut from the inside."

Seus didn't move. He lay there on the ice and looked up at the dark, ordinary sky, and she watched him put it together, piece by piece, the way you put together the pieces of something you dropped.

"So he left," he said. "Again."

She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything true to say that would help.

"You were in there." His voice was very flat. "You had it. All of it. In your hands."

"Yes."

"You could have fixed it."

"Yes."

"You could have brought them back. Aoi. Tom. Kuze. Dasha. All of them. You could have made it so none of it ever happened, and none of them would ever know."

"Yes," Val said.

"Then why—"

He came up off the ice. He had her by the collar before she saw him move, and he shoved her down flat onto the frost, and his hands were at her throat, where the Halo used to be. He wasn't squeezing. He was holding on, hard, shaking, the way you hold on to the edge of something when you're afraid of how far down it goes.

"Why didn't you—"

She could have gone quiet. Every other time in her life, she would have. She would have looked at the sky over his shoulder and said *okay* and let it happen, whatever it was, because it was safer to be nobody.

"Because then it would have been mine," she said. "All of it. Everybody. I'd have written every one of them, and they'd have woken up inside it and never known."

"So what?" His voice cracked straight down the middle. "So *what*. At least they'd be *here*."

"They can be here," Val said. "They have to want to."

His hands tightened. Not enough to hurt. Enough to know he could.

She reached up and put her hand on his face.

She did it the way Maggie had once put a hand on her ankle, on the floor of a narrow steel room, and left it there: not to stop anything, not to fix anything, only so there would be a hand. Her palm was against his cheek, against the frost on it, and she left it there.

He stopped.

His face came apart. It didn't happen all at once. It went from the edges in, the way frost goes off a window, and he made a sound she had never heard him make, and his tears fell on her face, hot, and ran down into the frost under her head. He didn't let go of her collar. He didn't push her down anymore, either. He just held on, and cried, and she lay on her back on the ice and let him, with her hand on his face.

After a long time, he said, thickly, "Your hand's freezing."

"Sorry."

She didn't take it away.

He didn't move away from it.

They sat up, eventually. They sat side by side on the lip of the shaft, a meter apart, and looked at the frost, and didn't say anything for a long time. It wasn't the kind of silence she used to keep. It wasn't empty. It was just two people who didn't have anything to say yet.

Far out on the plain, something moved.

A shape sat up out of the glittering ground, the way you sit up in bed when you've forgotten where you are. It was much too far to see who it was. It sat there, in the frost, for a long time, not moving, as if it were deciding something.

Then it stood up.

Val stood up too.

"Who is it?" Seus said.

"I don't know."

She lifted her hand anyway.

"Good morning," she called, across the frost.

Her voice went out over the plain, small and clear in the cold, and nothing answered it.

"It's not morning, Kane," Seus said.

"I know."

She said it again.

## Coda: Counting Up

> *Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.*
>
> — Psalm 30:5

Kasumi was still warm. That was the first thing. The creek still didn't freeze. The steam still came up between the shacks, and the frost of the world lay on the hills all around the valley and stopped at the edge of it, as if the valley had been told.

Nanami came back first, on the ninth day.

She walked up out of the frost by the little bridge with Hinata asleep in her arms and her hair full of light, and looked around at the empty square, and at Val and Seus coming down the hill at a run, and said, "What day is it?" And then, before anybody could answer: "Somebody has to count."

So she counted. Every morning, in the square, out loud, the way she had counted on the road out of the city with a hundred and twelve people behind her in the dark, except that now the numbers went the other way.

Haruto came back on the eleventh day, with his camera, and it took him a week to get the battery to charge. Akari came back on the twentieth. She asked about Kōta before she asked about anything else. He hadn't come back. He still hasn't. She goes down to the creek every evening, to the place by the bridge where the lanterns go out, and sits, and watches the frost on the far bank. Some evenings Val sits with her. A meter away.

The icebox in Nanami's kitchen still has the old sheet on it, the one with two columns, QUESTIONS and ANSWERS, four hundred and something tally marks under the first one and nothing under the second. Nobody takes it down. Next to it, Val pinned a new sheet. She wrote on the top of it, in small careful letters, with a pencil that wasn't her mother's:

*FOR APRIL. LATER.*

Underneath, she writes things down. Not every day. When there's something.

*The sun came out on day forty, for eleven minutes. It's bigger in real life. You were right to want to see it.*

*Hinata's first word was "no."*

*Seus learned to make rice. It's bad. He burns the bottom. Tom had a trick for that, and nobody ever found out what it was. We eat the burnt part too.*

*Alex hasn't come back. I don't know if he can. I made the omelettes anyway, at four in the morning, the way he did. They tore. I ate every bite this time.*

*Seus still doesn't say good morning. Most days he says "yeah." Once, he said "morning." Nanami wrote down the date.*

*I don't know if you'll ever ask. I'm going to keep writing them down anyway. That's the normal thing to do.*

This morning there was someone new in the square. A man nobody knew, with gray in his beard and the frost still melting off his coat, who had walked in out of the hills at dawn and stood at the edge of the steam as if he wasn't sure he was allowed.

Val saw him first. She crossed the square, and stopped in front of him, and said it before he could.

"Good morning."

He looked at her for a long moment, the way you look at a word you've forgotten how to say.

"...Morning," he said.

Behind her, Nanami lifted her clipboard and counted the square, out loud, one head at a time, the way she did every day.

"Twenty-eight," she said, and wrote it down.

It was one more than yesterday.
