#### Chapter 13: The Third War on Heaven

**Arc 54: Twelve**

Nobody briefed her. A technician came to her door in the morning with a clipboard and said, "Hangar zero, two hours," and left, and that was the mission.

She had told herself all night what she was going for. Not to undo anything. Abriel had said that on the tower, with her hand in his and the whole dead world in front of them: *You can't. I've tried.* She was going to answer a question. A question asked in a white room by a girl with short hair, still waiting somewhere on the other side of the seam for the answer Val hadn't given. She had said it to herself so many times in the dark that it had started to sound true.

Hangar zero was the deepest one, cut into the side of the shaft itself, so far down that the glow from the bottom lit the walls a faint, cold blue. The cradle hung out over the drop on steel arms, and in the cradle, waiting, was a unit she had never seen.

It was tall, taller than 04 had ever been, and narrow, and black. Not painted black: black all the way through, like glass that has been in a fire. Its arms were too long. Its head was smooth and small. Along its spine, where every other exo had one capsule, it had two, side by side, joined down the middle, so that two pilots would sit shoulder to shoulder inside it like passengers on a train. Its designation was painted on its shoulder in white: 12.

Abriel was already there, sitting on the edge of the cradle with his legs swinging over the shaft, as if it were the roof. He waved when he saw her.

Across the hangar, Unit 00 stood in its own cradle with its capsule open, and the girl with April's face stood in front of it in her flight suit, waiting.

"Valeria Kane," the girl said, when Val came near. "I will accompany you to the door."

Val looked at her for a moment. The short hair. Sam's eyes.

"Okay," she said.

That was all either of them said. It was the longest conversation they'd had.

A voice came over the hangar speakers, mild and even, from somewhere far above in the ruins. She had heard that voice read her the weather. She had heard it say *Be careful* at the end of a hundred briefings. She had heard it say *Lieutenant, Proxy authorization* in a port, once, without rising at all.

"Valeria," Patrick said. "Have you eaten?"

She stood under Unit 12 with her hands in her parka pockets and didn't answer. The speaker hissed for a while, as if he were waiting. Then: "Launch when ready."

Abriel climbed in first, and Val followed.

The double capsule was close and dim and smelled of new resin. The two seats sat side by side with no wall between them, so close that her shoulder touched his when she strapped in. The walls were dark. Abriel looked over at her and smiled, the same smile as the roof, as if this were just another night and they were just going to look at the sky.

"Ready?" he said.

She wasn't. She nodded.

The link opened.

It opened in both of them at once, and it was nothing like 04, nothing like Navis, nothing like the hollow's empty dark. It was like stepping into a river with someone else and feeling the current take you both. She felt Unit 12 around her, huge and cold and new. She felt Abriel beside her inside it, not as a voice or a pressure but as a presence, warm and very large, larger than he looked, much larger, like looking at a boy and seeing the shadow of a mountain behind him.

The Halo around her throat began to blink red, sensing the spike. A thin high tone started up under her jaw.

Abriel looked at it. He reached over and touched it with two fingers, lightly, the way you'd touch someone's new scarf.

"Commander Moriah gave me the code for this," he said. "He gives me anything I ask for. I could take it off you."

"Then take it off."

He seemed to think about it. Then he took his hand away.

"No," he said. "If I take it off, there's nobody holding the other end."

"He's never pressed it."

"I know," said Abriel, and smiled, as if that settled it. "That's why I'm leaving it."

She didn't understand him. She didn't ask. The tone went on under her jaw, thin and patient, and he took her hand instead and squeezed it, and looked up through the dark walls of the capsule toward the top of the shaft and the sky.

Unit 12 rose out of the ruins of headquarters and climbed toward the seam in the sky.

Sixty kilometers to the north, in a cloud bank over the mountains, the Vigil hung in the air on half power, the way it had hung there for twelve days.

Nobody aboard had asked why the commander kept the ship holding station within reach of the ruins of Hakobune, when there was a whole frozen world to hide in. Nobody had needed to ask.

On the bridge, at four in the afternoon, the small black switch on the corner of Alex's console chirped.

He picked it up. For twelve days its readout had shown almost nothing, only a faint carrier signal at the edge of its range, a heartbeat, steady, from somewhere inside the ruins. Now it was lit. The sync line on it was climbing. It went past the mark where Alex had set the soft limit months ago, and past the mark beyond that, and kept going, smoothly, as if there were no marks at all.

"Commander," said the sensor officer. "Something's coming up out of the ruins. One unit. Big. It's climbing. It's..." She stared at her screen. "It's going for the seam."

Alex looked at the switch in his hand. He didn't put it down.

"Seus," he said, on the open channel. His voice was perfectly calm. "Maggie. Launch."

**Arc 55: Undo**

The seam opened for them as if it had been waiting.

From the ground it had always been a line, a long bright crack down the middle of the sky. From inside Unit 12, climbing toward it, it was a door. It was taller than mountains and thinner than a hair, and along its edges the light didn't fall out of it so much as lean out, curious, like someone looking through a gap in a curtain. The closer they came, the more it seemed to widen to meet them, without moving, the way a face seems to come closer when it smiles.

They climbed through the red haze and came out above it, and for a moment, before the door filled everything, there were stars: real ones, hard and small and close, more of them than she had ever seen from the roof.

"Look," Abriel said, delighted, exactly the way he said it on the roof. "You can see the Pencil from up here."

She looked. It was there, low and crooked, three in a row. He looked as pleased as if he'd named it himself.

She had come to answer a question. She held on to that. But the closer they got to the door, the more she wanted something else.

She wanted to go back. She wanted to undo the white room, the light, the districts lifting, the shelter under the school. She wanted the morning before. She wanted Akari's punch and Nanami's good mornings and Haruto's camera in her face and Seus in the apartment next door complaining through the wall. She wanted the seventh day back, the first day back, a hangar full of strangers and a child's voice asking her for a name. She wanted it so much that it hurt in the link, a pressure in the chest of Unit 12 like a held breath, and Unit 12 climbed faster, as if her wanting were fuel.

Halfway to the door, she understood with horrible clarity exactly who she sounded like.

*You'd burn the sky to see your daughter again.*

She had said that to a voice in Navis's walls, in a corridor turned to frost, to a man who had waited twenty-seven years to go back to one morning. A man who would burn the sky to see his daughter again. She had torn the core out of him for wanting this. She had killed him for wanting this, and now she was him.

Her hand tightened on Abriel's. He didn't say anything. He was looking up at the door, and his face was very calm, and very far away.

Below them, on the frozen plain, three units came out of the dark.

The first came fast and ugly, flying low over the ice on jets that coughed and stuttered: a patched-together thing, mismatched plates welded over mismatched bones, three hollows' worth of white armor and the long gray arms of some second-generation unit nobody had wanted. Its welds still glowed from the yard. Its paint wasn't dry. It had been built in four days around a core that a boy had carried down from orbit in his arms, and it was flying because that boy had kicked it until it flew.

"Kane!" Seus was screaming over the open channel, his voice cracking with the cold and the speed. "Kane! Turn around, you idiot! That's not a door, it's a mouth!"

Val's breath stopped.

He was alive. She had known that for weeks. She had sat on the floor of the Vigil's bay with him standing over her. But she had not heard his voice say her name like that, high and raw and terrified, since a port, since a comm channel, since *It's okay, Val, it's okay.* He was coming after her.

"How do you know?" she said into the channel. It came out a whisper.

"Because it's laughing!" Seus shouted. "The thing in my eye! It knows what that is! It's laughing, Kane, and it's hungry! Turn around!"

The second unit came behind him, very old, rattling, orange under its scorch marks, climbing in long patient arcs. Its pilot was humming. It was something old, from before the white sky, and she hummed it the whole way up, and between the bars, very gently, she said, "Kiddo. Kiddo, come on down now. Come on."

The third unit came up out of the ruins behind Unit 12, white, with no number painted on it anywhere. Zero-Four, on her commander's orders, coming to make sure they went through.

Unit 00 turned in the air and dropped between Unit 12 and the two units climbing toward it, and spread its arms.

"Get out of my way!" Seus screamed at it.

"My orders are to ensure they reach the door," said Zero-Four. Her voice on the channel was perfectly flat. It was Val's voice, almost, at nine.

"Your orders are garbage! Your commander is garbage! You're a— you're a copy of a—" Seus's patched unit slammed into Unit 00 in midair, and the two of them tumbled, white and gray, end over end above the plain. "Move!"

"Seus, go!" Maggie's unit came in from the side and caught Unit 00 by the arm and wrenched it away from him, still humming, a little breathless now. "The white one's mine. Go get her!"

He went. He came up out of the tumble and climbed, coughing jets and all, toward Unit 12, and Unit 12 kept climbing away from him toward the door, and the door kept widening without moving.

Inside the capsule, Val looked at Abriel.

"He came after me," she said.

"He did," said Abriel.

"He hates me."

Abriel turned his head and looked at her, and his face was gentle and sad and very old.

"People don't climb into the sky after people they hate," he said.

Val looked at the channel display, at Seus's call sign blinking on it, getting closer, getting closer. She could stop. She knew she could. She could take her hands off the controls, or say *turn around* to the boy beside her, and Unit 12 would turn, and she would go down to the plain and let Seus scream at her for the rest of her life.

Or she could go through, and undo it. And if she undid it, he would never hate her. He would never die in a port. He would never wake up with a patch on his eye and burns on his neck and a father who looked at him like a clerical error. None of it would happen. Nobody would ever have to climb into the sky after anyone.

She chose. She knew she was choosing, this time. Nobody else's button. Nobody else's hand.

She kept climbing.

Abriel didn't stop her. He only said, very quietly, almost to himself, "You always do." There was no blame in it at all.

It was a trap. It had always been a trap. The people above Umbral had needed someone to open the door from inside, someone who wanted it badly enough, with sync high enough, sitting next to the one thing in the world the door would open all the way for. And here she was, doing it for them.

Unit 12 touched the seam.

The real Third War on Heaven began.

The door opened. Not the half-open seam the Almost had left: wider, and wider, splitting the sky from the hills to the top of the world, as if someone were drawing back a curtain the size of the sky. What came through it this time wasn't light. It was cold. A cold so deep it had a sound, a high singing like a finger run around the rim of a glass, and where it touched the sky, the sky turned to glass. Val watched it happen through Unit 12's eyes. The red haze over the world went clear and hard and then cracked, in long white lines, like ice on a pond when someone steps on it. The clouds froze where they hung and fell, slowly, in pieces, a rain of crystal the size of houses, turning in the light as they came down.

On the cracked Moon, Sariel opened its eyes.

Val saw it. Everyone on Earth who was looking up saw it. In the long red crack down the face of the Moon, two lights opened, very far apart, very slowly, the way a sleeper opens their eyes in the morning and doesn't yet know where they are. They looked down. They looked down at the world, and at the door, and at the black unit hanging in it.

Then the flesh began to fall.

It came down over the frozen plain like snow: soft, pale, drifting flakes of something that wasn't snow. Saint flesh. Pieces of the belt, pieces of the Moon, pieces of the old dead fathers in the sky, shaken loose by the cold and falling to Earth in their billions, settling on the ice, on the ruins, on Seus's patched armor, like snow made of skin.

And through the link, through the door, through the cold, Val felt them wake. All across the solar system, in the belt, under the ice of Europa, deep in the stone of the Moon, the Nephilim that had not yet fallen began to stir inside their fathers. Thousands of them. She felt them the way you feel a crowd behind a wall: thousands of small, slow heartbeats starting up in the dark, one after another, like children waking in a dormitory at the sound of a bell. They turned toward the door. They turned toward home.

On the plain, a wave of the cold came down off the door like a wind off a glacier and hit the three units below.

Seus's patched unit was thrown across the plain like a toy, tumbling, shedding plates, and came down on its back in the snow of flesh, and lay still. Lizzie went down skidding, sideways, a long orange streak across the ice, throwing up crystal, and Maggie's humming cut off on the channel mid-bar. Unit 00 was struck out of the air by a falling shard of frozen cloud the size of a train, and broke, and fell, and its pilot fell with it.

The channel went quiet. Not silent. Quiet: a hiss, empty, very wide.

The Almost had broken the world. The Third War broke Val.

**Arc 56: Final Form**

The Halo at her throat was blinking.

It had been blinking slowly since the hangar, a patient red, in time with her heartbeat. Now it blinked faster. And faster. The tone came back, high and thin, not under her jaw now but everywhere, filling the capsule, because she wanted, and the door was taking everything she wanted and turning it into width.

Beside her, Abriel was changing.

She could feel it through the link. His sync had gone past anything Unit 12 had been built to measure: past human, past exo, past the line where the numbers on the walls simply stopped and turned into symbols Val didn't know. Something enormous was unfolding inside him, a shape far too large for a body. It was like being in a small room with someone and feeling them begin to stand up, and keep standing up, and keep standing up, until their head was through the ceiling and their shoulders were through the roof and they were still standing up.

Outside, through Unit 12's eyes, she saw it happen to the unit too. Crystal was growing down its black arms, white and branching, like frost racing across a windowpane. Its fingers lengthened. Over its small smooth head, a shape was forming, pale and blank and beautiful, like a mask carved out of ice. She had seen that mask before. She had seen it on giants falling over the river, over the harbor, over the plant. She had broken a hundred of them.

The capsule was getting cold.

Frost was forming on the walls, spreading from Abriel's side of the capsule toward hers in long white ferns. Her breath was smoking. His wasn't. His skin had gone pale and faintly clear at the edges, like the second skin of ice on a puddle, and under it, very faintly, there was light.

"It's all right," he told her from the seat beside hers.

His voice sounded farther away than it should have. As if he were speaking from the bottom of a well, or the top of a stair.

"I was always going to go back." He smiled at her. The light under his skin moved when he smiled. "I'm the last one, Val. The last child, and the last name in their book. The door needs one of us to open the rest of the way."

"You knew," Val said. "You knew what it was."

"I always know." He shook his head slowly, and frost fell out of his hair, and he was still smiling. "I come anyway. Every time."

"Why?"

"Because you come," Abriel said, as if it were obvious. "And I'd rather be here."

"Then stop. Stop it, turn around, we can—"

"I can't stop it. It's already got hold of me." He looked down at his hand, at the light moving under the skin. "It wants a giant. If I go through as that, it opens all the way, for all of them, and the sky comes down for good. If I go as me, it's only a boy going home. It doesn't open for a boy."

"Then go as you."

"I never manage it." He said it simply, without any pity for himself, the way he'd once said the sea was salt. "Every time, it gets too big. Every time, I end up as that."

She understood, then. All at once, the way you understand that a step is missing in the dark. Every time. Every turn of whatever this was, this was the moment: this seat, this cold. And every time he ended up as the thing she had spent seven years breaking, and the door opened all the way, and the stairs began again.

"No," Val said.

"Val."

"No. No, no, no. It's mine." The word came out of her like something torn. She had never said it. Not about anything. Not luck, not the plan, not 04. "I did this. You said so, on the tower. It's mine. I opened it. Take me instead, let it be me, give it to me—"

"It can't be you," Abriel said gently. "You're not one of us. It was never going to be you." He smiled again. "I didn't come up here to fix anything, Val. I just wanted to see the sky with someone first."

The frost reached her seat.

It came over the armrest and onto her hand, and it burned, the way cold burns, white and sudden. Abriel saw it and his face changed for the first time, a flicker of something like fear, not for himself.

"Let go," he said. "Val. You'll freeze. Let go of me."

She thought of a transport manifest, and a callsign crossed out in small neat handwriting, and a page folded under a pillow.

She didn't let go.

She tore the straps off her shoulders and climbed out of her seat and into his, into the cold, and put her arms around him.

"Oh," Abriel said softly, and he sounded, for the first time since she had known him, surprised. "You've never done that before."

The cold went into her all at once. It went through her flight suit and her skin and into her bones, into her teeth, behind her eyes. Her eyelashes froze. Her hair went stiff with frost. Her fingers went white, then numb, then nothing. And still she held on, with her arms around a boy who was turning into the thing she had spent seven years killing, and she didn't let go.

"Everybody who stands next to me dies," she said into his shoulder. Her lips were stiff. The words came out in pieces. "Everybody. You know that, right?"

"I know," said Abriel. "I picked the seat anyway."

Through the link, she felt the enormous shape inside him pause.

It didn't stop. It was still unfolding, still standing up through the ceiling and the roof and the sky. But it paused, the way a tide pauses at the top before it turns, because something small and stubborn and frozen was holding on to the boy at the bottom of it.

"Oh," Abriel said again, softer. "That helps. Hold on, then. Hold on to me, and I'll stay me." He laughed, a little, and his breath didn't smoke at all. "I want to go as me. Not as that."

So she held on.

Sixty kilometers to the north, on the Vigil's bridge, the sync line on the small black switch had gone off the top of its scale.

Alex held it in his palm with his thumb resting on it, not pressing, the way he had held it for months. On the main screen, the long-range cameras showed the door, wide open now from the hills to the top of the world, and the black unit hanging in the middle of it with something white growing over its face.

"Commander." The sensor officer's voice was shaking. "The collar. If you cut her link... it might stop it. It might close."

Nobody on the bridge said anything. Everyone was looking at his hand.

*Would you sacrifice the city for one life?* a mild voice had asked him once, across an ops room, with a boy screaming over the speakers. He had stood down. He had chosen the city. And years later, under a beam, a woman with dust on her hands had held his face and told him to say no to her, one more time, and he had.

His thumb hung a millimeter above the switch.

"No," Alex said.

He said it to the sensor officer, and to the mild voice in the ops room, and to the sky. Then he closed his hand around the switch so that his thumb was nowhere near it, and held it against his chest, and watched the screen.

In the capsule, she held on.

She held on while the frost climbed her arms and her shoulders and her neck. She held on while the light under his skin got brighter and the mask over Unit 12's face finished forming, white and smooth and blank. She held on while the Halo's tone rose and rose at her throat, until it wasn't a tone anymore but a single held note, like the cold singing out of the door. She held on to him the way she had held April in the white room, with everything, and this time she knew what she was holding and she held it anyway.

He put his hand on the back of her frozen head, very gently.

"Okay," Abriel said quietly, the way you'd say it at the top of a long flight of stairs. "I'm going now. As me."

"No—"

"I'm going to remember this one," he said. "See you next time around."

He let go.

Not of her. Of the other thing: the enormous shape standing up through the sky. She felt him put it down, the way you'd set down something you've carried so long you forgot it was heavy. The mask over Unit 12's face cracked straight down the middle. The crystal on its arms went still. The shape inside him simply wasn't there anymore, the way a shadow isn't there when the light goes out.

He let out a breath, a long one, like someone reaching the top of a stair.

And then he wasn't there either.

He came apart in her arms. Not all at once: softly, from the edges in, the way frost comes off a window when the sun hits it. His hair first, into a fine bright powder. Then his shoulders. Then the weight of his head. He went up out of her arms as frost and light, through the open top of the capsule, up into the cold, rising, like snow falling in reverse, toward the door. Toward the Moon. Toward home.

Val held the place where he had been.

Above them, the door stopped. It hung wide open for a moment longer, very still, as if listening for someone who wasn't coming. It had been waiting for a giant. What came up to it was a boy. Then, slowly, from the edges in, it began to close. It drew itself together across the sky, the way a wound closes, until there was only a seam again, and then less than a seam: a long pale scar from the hills to the top of the world, faintly lit, healed over, not gone.

The crystal stopped falling. The flesh stopped falling. On the Moon, the two lights in the crack went dim, and then dark. In the belt and under the ice, the small heartbeats slowed, and slowed, and went still again, unfinished.

Unit 12 fell out of the sky with nobody flying it.

It came down onto the frozen plain on its back, hard, and slid a long way, and stopped.

Sixty kilometers to the north, the line on the small black switch had spiked once, higher than it had ever gone, at the moment he came apart in her arms. Alex's hand closed tighter around it. Then the line fell, and fell, and fell, and steadied, very low and very faint. A heartbeat. Hers.

He hadn't pressed it. He looked at it in his hand for a long time, at the small faint line that was still moving. Then he sat down where he was, on the floor of the bridge, in front of the whole crew, and nobody said anything.

An hour later the storm of falling crystal reached the mountains, tore the Vigil's port flight surfaces off, and drove the ship north, blind, for two hundred kilometers. By the time anyone had it under control again, the carrier signal had slid off the edge of the switch's range, and there was nothing on any channel from the plain but static.

When it was over, the sky was dark, darker than it had been in seven years, and the world was colder than it had been, and quieter. Val sat in the ruin of Unit 12, in the seat that wasn't hers, with her arms still frozen in the shape of holding someone and her hand still reaching toward the empty air in front of her, and something in her finally gave way, all the way down to the bottom.

**Arc 57: The Walk**

They found each other on the frozen plain afterward: three survivors in a dead world.

Seus came first, limping out of his ruined unit, bleeding from the scalp and still furious.

His patched armor lay on its back in the snow of flesh with its chest split open and steam coming out of the split. He climbed out of the capsule into a cold that took his breath away, a new cold, a worse one, and stood there in the soft pale drifts with the blood running down into his good eye. He wiped it away with his sleeve. He tried his comms. Nothing. He tried the WAKE frequency, the emergency band, Maggie's channel. Nothing. Static, and under the static, faintly, something that sounded like singing and wasn't.

He looked up. There was no sign of the Vigil anywhere in the sky. There was no sign of anything. The sky was dark and scarred and empty, and the Moon had gone dull, like a coal going out.

"Great," Seus said, to nobody. "Great. That's great."

He found Unit 12 by the long scar it had cut across the ice. It lay at the end of it, black and broken, with crystal frozen in branches down one arm and a white mask split down the middle over its face. He climbed up its side, slipping, swearing, and got the capsule open with a piece of broken plating.

Val was inside. She was sitting in the wrong seat, the left one, with frost all over her: in her hair, on her eyelashes, in a white crust over her flight suit. Her arms were up in front of her, curved, frozen in place in the shape of holding someone. There was nobody in them. At her throat, the collar blinked once, slowly, red, looking for a signal that wasn't there.

Seus stared at her.

"Kane," he said.

She didn't move.

He had to break the ice off her sleeves to get her arms down. He did it with the heel of his hand, swearing, chipping it off in white flakes, and when her arms finally came down they just dropped into her lap and lay there. He hauled her out by the back of her flight suit. She didn't resist. She didn't do anything. Her eyes were open, and there was no one behind them.

He dragged her down off Unit 12's side and set her on her feet in the snow of flesh, and she stood, the way a coat stands when you hang it on a hook, and he let go, and she sat down.

"Get up," Seus said. "Get up. Walk."

She didn't.

"Kane. I'm serious. Get up. It's minus forty and dropping and nobody's coming, do you understand me? Nobody is coming. Alex isn't coming. Maggie's—" He stopped. He looked across the plain toward the long orange streak on the ice, a kilometer off, where Lizzie had gone down. Nothing moved there. "Nobody is coming. Get up."

She didn't.

He swore, slung her arm over his shoulder, and walked for both of them.

He picked south, because the scar in the sky ran north, and he wanted it behind him.

A little way behind them came the girl from Unit 00. She had climbed out of her own wreck with her flight suit torn down one side and a cut across her forehead that she didn't seem to have noticed. Now she followed them across the ice at a distance, Sam Kane's face expressionless, because no one had given her any other orders.

Seus noticed her after the first hour. He stopped and turned around, with Val hanging off his shoulder.

"Stop following us," he said.

The girl stopped. "I have no orders."

"Then go somewhere."

"I have no orders to go somewhere."

Seus looked at her for a long time. The cut on her forehead. The torn suit. The face, which was so nearly Val's face that it made his teeth hurt.

"Fine," he said. "Then follow us. Whatever."

He turned around and kept walking. After a moment, the girl kept following.

He talked. He couldn't help it. He was carrying the person he hated most in the world across a frozen plain under a dark sky, and her feet dragged in the snow, and she didn't say anything, and if he didn't talk he would have to listen to the silence and the static that sounded like singing. So he talked.

"You don't get to do this," he told her. "You hear me? You don't get to just stop. I stopped once. I died. It's not restful. It's a year and a half of nothing and then you wake up and everyone's older and your exo's dead and your father has you escorted out of the building." He hitched her arm higher on his shoulder. "So you don't get to do it. Pick up your feet."

She didn't pick up her feet.

"You know what Alex said to me, when I woke up? The first thing? He said *welcome back*. Like I'd been on vacation. Like I'd gone to the beach." Seus laughed, and it hurt his ribs. "And then he said sorry. He's always saying sorry. You ever notice that? Sorry, sorry, sorry, and never for what." He walked. "You'd have liked my exo. Navis. It used to ask me if things were good. *Was that good?* After everything. After a fight. After a stupid dance on a harbor. And I never answered it, because I'm an idiot, because I thought there'd be time. And now it's dead. You killed it, forcing your way in. Or I did, a long time before that, by never answering it. I go back and forth." He walked. "Pick up your feet, Kane."

She didn't.

The dark came early. It always came early now. When it came, he found them a place: a piece of one of the hanging districts that had fallen, sometime in the last seven years, and stuck in the plain at an angle, half buried. An apartment block, upside down. He kicked in a window and dragged Val through it, and they were standing on a ceiling, with the light fixtures sticking up out of the floor around them like strange plants, and the furniture all bolted to the roof over their heads, a table and four chairs hanging upside down in the dark like bats.

He broke one of the chairs down off the ceiling and made a fire out of it on the floor that had been a ceiling, in a bowl that had been a lampshade. He put Val next to it. She sat where he put her and looked at the fire with nobody behind her eyes, and he took off his WAKE jacket and put it around her, and swore at her for making him cold.

The girl from Unit 00 came in through the window after him and stood just inside it, at the edge of the firelight, with her arms at her sides.

"You can sit," Seus said.

"I have not been ordered to sit."

He stared at her. "Sit."

She sat, instantly, cross-legged on the ceiling, her back very straight.

"You didn't have to wait for me to say it," Seus said. "You can just sit. When you're cold. When you're tired. You don't need anybody to tell you."

The girl looked at him with Sam Kane's eyes. "I do not understand."

"Yeah," said Seus. "Me neither, most of the time."

He dug a ration bar out of the pocket of his flight suit, broke it in three, and pushed one piece into Val's frozen hand, where it stayed, and held out another toward the girl.

"I have not been ordered to eat," the girl said.

"Eat."

She ate it, in small, exact bites, looking at nothing.

"Next time," Seus said, "just eat it."

"If ordered," said the girl.

Seus looked at the two of them, the girl who wouldn't move and the girl who wouldn't stop following, one with his enemy's face and one with his enemy's father's face, sitting on a ceiling by a fire made out of a chair, and he put his head in his hands.

"Great," he said into his palms. "I'm a commander now."

He didn't sleep. Somebody had to keep the fire going, and somebody had to make sure Val kept breathing, and somebody had to watch the window in case something came through it. So he sat up all night, with his back against the upside-down wall and the cold in his bones, watching the two of them in the firelight, and every so often he put another piece of chair on the fire.

"Somebody has to sit up with the world," he muttered, around three in the morning, in a fair imitation of Alex's voice, and laughed at himself, and then didn't.

Toward dawn, the broken window went gray.

The girl from Unit 00 hadn't moved all night. Seus had his eyes half shut and a chair leg in his hand, ready for the fire. Val sat where he'd put her, beside the ashes, with his jacket around her shoulders and nobody behind her eyes.

The girl turned her head and looked at Val.

"Good morning," she said.

Seus's eyes came open.

Nobody had ordered it. He knew that, because he was the only one left who gave her orders, and he hadn't. She said it carefully, the way you'd read a word off a card you weren't sure was the right one. Somebody, Seus thought, had said that to her. Every morning. Until it stuck.

Val didn't answer. She looked at the ashes.

The girl waited a moment, as if there were a procedure for what came next and she couldn't find it. Then she turned back to the window and the gray light, and stood by.

Seus looked at the two of them for a long time.

"Yeah," he said finally, roughly, to nobody. "Morning."

Then he got Val up again. He slung her arm over his shoulder again. He climbed out through the window into the gray, and the girl followed.

Three figures walked south across a frozen world, under a dark sky, with the scar at their backs.
