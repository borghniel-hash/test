#### Chapter 12: Not the Same

**Arc 50: Who Is April?**

Unit 00 flew all night, and the girl who flew it never said a word.

The capsule was built for one. Val sat wedged in behind the pilot's seat with her knees against the curved wall and the Halo cold against her throat, and the girl sat in front of her with her hands on the controls, perfectly still, flying the way 04 used to walk patrols in the first years, by protocol, without a single wasted motion. The walls showed the night outside: black mountains, then black plains, then frost, white and endless under the red Moon. Every so often a light fell slowly across the sky, a piece of the Morningstar still coming down years later, burning up on its way.

Val didn't look at any of it. She looked at the back of the girl's head. The short hair. The shape of the ear. The line of the jaw when the girl turned, once, to check something on the wall.

She had a thousand things to say. She had said all of them once, in a white room, in order, kneeling, with a small weight in her arms. Now she couldn't find the first one. She was afraid that if she said anything at all, the girl would turn around and be someone else, or would turn around and not be there, and Val would wake up on the floor of the Vigil's bay under a crackling blanket with Alex walking away from her.

She had answered seven questions in a white room. Now she couldn't ask one.

So she didn't. She sat behind the girl with April's face and watched her fly, and that was the whole night.

They came down at dawn into the ruins of Hakobune.

Val had seen the city from the back of a salvage truck, from the street, looking up. She had never seen it from the air. The districts that hung over it were bigger than she had understood, whole neighborhoods turned upside down and frozen in place, trailing icicles hundreds of meters long, and under them the lower city lay buried in white like something sleeping under a sheet. The seam in the sky pulsed overhead, slow and faint. In the middle of the ruins, where headquarters had stood, there was a hole.

It was round. It went straight down. It was lined with frost, and at the bottom of it, so far down that the dawn light didn't reach, something glowed: cold, pale, slow, like a heartbeat seen through ice.

Val knew that glow. She had seen it the night the world ended, from the lip of the same hole, with Navis's hands full of her own exo.

Unit 00 dropped past the lip of the shaft and down along its frozen side to a ledge where the old levels of headquarters broke off into open air, and landed in what had once been a hangar. The capsule opened. Cold air came in, and voices.

Patrick's Umbral lived in the ruins of headquarters, in the lower levels that had survived. It was cold and quiet and full of people who didn't look up.

They didn't look up when Val came down off Unit 00's leg. They didn't look up as two guards in gray walked her through the hangar and into the corridors. The corridors were patched with plastic sheeting where the walls had cracked, and the sheeting breathed in and out with the draft from the shaft. The lights ran at half power. Technicians in heavy coats sat at consoles with their breath smoking in the air, typing, and none of them turned their heads. Frost grew on the inside of the walls in long fern shapes, like the frost that used to grow on the Reliquary doors, as if the cold were coming from inside the building now, from everywhere.

Somebody had pinned a shift schedule to a pillar. Somebody had taped a child's drawing to a door. Those were the only signs that people lived here.

Patrick met her in what was left of his office.

It had been on the forty-first floor once, with a window over the harbor. Now it was in the basement, in a room that had been a records vault, with the walls lined in empty steel shelves and a desk that didn't belong to it. There was one lamp. There were reports stacked at his left hand, the way there always had been, and a pen. Behind the desk, leaning against the wall where a window would have been, was a single blank canvas, white, untouched, with a fine skin of frost across it.

He had aged too, though less than Alex, as if he had decided aging was a waste of time. His hair had gone gray at the sides. His coat was the same coat, or another one exactly like it. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it had always done for her and for no one else, softening slightly, the way a cold room softens when someone opens a door.

"Valeria," he said.

She didn't say anything.

He was exactly the same as before: warm to her, polite, unreadable. He told her she was needed. He told her nothing about what for. When she asked, he said, "You'll be told," in the same mild voice he used to use for *the car's outside* and *your bento's on the counter*, and went back to his reports.

Then, the way he always had at the gate, he asked if she had eaten.

Val stood in front of his desk with her hands at her sides and looked at him. Seven years. The sky. The port. Seus. And he wanted to know if she'd had breakfast.

"The girl in Unit 00," she said.

"She's useful," Patrick said, and moved on, to a report about the shaft's temperature, which he read aloud to her for some reason, the way he used to read her the weather.

Val realized, with a chill, that he didn't care about the girl at all. He didn't say it coldly. That was the worst part. He said *she's useful* the way you'd say *the car runs* or *the lights work*, a fact about equipment, and then he turned the page.

"What about Seus?" she said.

Patrick's pen stopped.

"He came to you," Val said. "After he woke up. He walked eleven days across the ice to get here. He told me. He stood right here, and you had security walk him out."

Patrick looked at the pen for a while. Then he capped it, and set it down beside the reports, very precisely, parallel to the edge of the desk.

"He's an adult now," he said. "He can think for himself."

And he stood, and picked up his coat, and left her standing in the vault.

Seus was sixteen. He would be sixteen forever, with his eye behind a patch and his burns still shiny on his neck. Patrick knew that better than anyone alive. Val stood in front of the empty desk and the blank canvas and understood that he had said it anyway, on purpose, the way you'd close a door.

It was no different from before. It was worse, because now Val knew what he had done to his own son, and he still treated her like the one daughter he had.

But at least April was here.

They gave her a room on the level below, a narrow concrete box with a cot and a lamp and a vent that breathed cold air from the shaft. They gave her rations, the same gray rice and pickled radish as Ararat, and a heavy gray parka with a hood, Umbral issue, too big for her, smelling of storage. Nobody mentioned the Halo. A technician looked at it once in the corridor, said, "WAKE hardware," to no one in particular, and walked on.

She kept the hood up everywhere she went, so nobody had to look at her face, and so she didn't have to watch them looking.

She found the girl in the hangar, standing in front of Unit 00, as still as a statue.

The white unit stood in its cradle with its capsule open, and the girl stood below it with her hands at her sides and her face turned up toward it, not doing anything. Not working on it. Not looking at anything on it. Just standing, the way a machine stands in a corner when it's switched off.

Val came up beside her and pushed back her hood.

"Hey," she said. Her voice came out wrong, too soft. She tried again. "Hey... April."

The girl turned her head.

"Who... is April?"

Val swallowed. "Your name is April, right?"

The girl looked at her. Up close, in the half light of the hangar, the face was even closer to Val's own than it had seemed in the wind on the Vigil: the same brow, the same eyes, the jaw that Val had seen on talk shows and old posters, softened. A face that had been made out of two faces.

"My designation is Zero-Four," the girl said. "I was made to serve Samuel Kane. Commander Moriah serves in his place."

"No," Val said. "No. You're April. I named you. In the white room, you asked me, on the first day you asked me for a name and I didn't give you one, and then I did. I gave it to you. You said it back to me. April. You said it twice."

"I have no record of a white room."

"You asked me why the sky was blue." Val heard her own voice climbing and couldn't stop it. "You asked me about my hair. You asked me if I had a mom. You asked if you'd done something wrong. You asked me if I was a hero. You had one more question. You always had one more question."

The girl considered this. She tilted her head very slightly, the way a camera adjusts its focus.

"I do not ask questions," she said.

She looked at Val with Sam's eyes, without recognition, without anything.

"April isn't my designation."

Val put out her hand. She didn't know what she meant to do with it. Touch the girl's face, maybe. Touch her hair, the short hair, cut the way Val had once told a girl in a white room that her mother wore it. The girl didn't step back. She didn't lean away. She didn't do anything at all. She let Val's hand stop in the air a few centimeters from her cheek, and waited, politely, for whatever this was to be over.

That was worse than anything. Worse than if she'd flinched.

Val took her hand back.

So that was it. Val had failed after all. She thought she understood, finally, what had happened in the white room. She had wanted 04 back with everything in her, and the door had heard. It had made a body out of what she wanted, and the body had come out wrong: a copy with her face and her father's, who had never asked her a single question. The April she'd named, the one she'd held, wasn't here. Val didn't know where she was.

"Is there something you require, Valeria Kane?" the girl asked.

"No," Val said.

"Then I will continue to stand by."

She turned back to Unit 00 and lifted her face to it again, and stood.

Val went back to the room they'd given her, lay on the bed, and stared at the ceiling. The vent breathed cold air down on her face. Somewhere far below, the glow at the bottom of the shaft beat once, slowly, and she felt it in the floor.

Everyone on WAKE hated her. Seus hated her. Her friends were dead. April was gone.

*04 did this*, she told herself. And there was no one left who looked at her the way they used to.

**Arc 51: Abriel**

She couldn't sleep, so on the third night she went up.

The stairwells in the ruins of headquarters went up a long way past anything that was still a building. She climbed until the concrete walls on either side of her gave way to open air and wind, and then she climbed some more, and came out through a twisted fire door onto a roof that wasn't a roof anymore. It was the top of whatever was left: a broad, cracked platform, half of it gone, open on three sides to the night. From up here you could see the whole frozen city, the hanging districts turning in the dark, the seam pulsing overhead like a vein, and over all of it, low and huge, the red Moon.

Someone was already there.

He stood at the edge, looking up: a boy with pale hair in a white Umbral flight suit, his hands in his pockets, his weight on his heels, perfectly relaxed with a forty-story drop in front of his toes. He heard her on the stairs and turned around, and he smiled as if she were exactly who he had been waiting for.

"Oh," he said. "It's you. I've been waiting for you, Val."

She knew his voice.

She had heard it from the inside, through his own ears, at the top of a broken tower at the end of the world. She had heard it say *04 did this* and then cut out, half a breath before whatever came next. She had carried that sentence for months. She had held on to it in the Vigil's bay and on the shelter steps and in a hollow's capsule with Dasha dead on the concrete. He was the one whose eyes she had looked through. He was the voice she trusted without knowing why.

She waited for him to look at her the way everyone looked at her now, like a fire that might still be burning.

He didn't. He came over and sat down on the edge of the roof, close, his legs hanging over the drop, as if they had known each other for years. After a moment, because her legs had gone strange, she sat down beside him.

"You're not afraid of me," she said.

"No, why would I be?"

"Everyone else is."

"Hmm, I guess that would make sense." He looked up at the Moon. He had a way of looking at things as if he had just been told they existed and was delighted to find out it was true. "I'm the one who stopped it, you know. That night. I came down from the Morningstar and put the Sword through it." He held up one hand, fingers together, and drew it slowly down through the air, a long straight line from the sky to the city, the way a child draws a falling star. "To stop it, I had to touch it."

He turned his head and smiled at her sideways.

"So I touched you. You were holding someone you loved, and you were so happy. I don't think monsters are happy like that."

Val didn't say anything. She couldn't. Something had closed in her throat, right under the Halo, and wouldn't open.

He seemed to understand that. He didn't wait for an answer. He reached into the pocket of his flight suit and took out a can, small and warm, and set it on the concrete between them. Sweet coffee. The konbini kind, with the cartoon cow on the label, the kind that came in pairs on a dashboard on quiet afternoons.

She stared at it.

"I asked the man who runs this place what you liked," he said. "He said that. He didn't even have to think about it." He tilted his head. "He knows a lot about you, for someone who doesn't talk to you."

She picked up the can. It was warm through her gloves. She didn't open it. She held it in both hands in her lap, and the warmth went into her fingers, and she sat there holding it while the boy beside her watched the Moon.

"How do you know my name?" she said finally.

"Everyone knows your name." He smiled. "I knew it before that."

"Before what?"

He didn't answer. His name was Abriel, he said. He was the newest pilot under Commander Moriah's command, and he had come from the Moon.

"Nobody comes from the Moon," said Val.

"I did," said Abriel, as if that settled it.

He didn't explain more than that, and Val didn't ask. She was too tired to ask, and she found, sitting there, that she didn't want to. She didn't want this to turn out to be anything but what it was: a boy on a roof, a warm can, and someone who looked at her and didn't see the end of the world.

He was the first person since she woke who looked at her like that.

They sat on the edge of the roof until the Moon had moved a hand's width across the sky. He didn't talk much. Neither did she. When the cold finally drove her back toward the stairs, he called after her, not getting up.

"Come back tomorrow?"

She stopped in the broken doorway with the can in her hands.

"Maybe," she said.

"I'll be here," Abriel said. He turned back to the Moon. "I'm good at waiting."

In her room, she opened the can and drank it, cold by then, too sweet, all of it.

**Arc 52: The Moon**

It became a ritual: every night, the roof and the red Moon.

She would come up the long stairs after the ruins had gone quiet, and he would already be there, at the edge, looking up, with two cans in his pockets. He never asked where she'd been during the day. She never told him. She would sit down beside him, and he would hand her a can, and they would watch the sky.

The sky over Hakobune was never dark, exactly. The veil of debris that had hung over the world since the Almost caught the light of the cracked Moon and glowed with it, a dim red haze from horizon to horizon. Through the gaps in it, here and there, real stars showed, very small and very far. And all night long, slow lights crossed it: pieces of the Morningstar, still falling, years later, burning up one by one in long patient lines, like matches struck very far away.

On the second night, Abriel started naming them.

"That one's the Ladle," he said, pointing at a cluster of stars that didn't look like a ladle at all. "That's the Sleeping Dog. And that long one, the one falling right now, is the Boy Who Forgot His Umbrella."

"Those aren't constellations," said Val.

"They are now. I named them." He looked delighted with himself. "Nobody else was using them. Your turn."

Val went very still.

*Your turn.* Small neat handwriting, pencil, on a page folded under a pillow.

Abriel noticed. He didn't ask. He looked at her for a moment, and then looked back at the sky, and waited, the way Sae used to wait on the hangar steps, a meter away, knees up, saying nothing. The falling light crossed half the sky and went out.

"Someone left me a note once," Val said. "That said that."

"Was it a good note?"

She thought about it for a long time.

"It was the only one I ever got," she said.

Abriel nodded slowly, as if that were a complete answer, and maybe it was. After a while, Val lifted one hand and pointed at a narrow line of stars low over the hills, three in a row, slightly crooked.

"That one's the Pencil," she said.

He laughed. It was a real laugh, startled and bright, the kind of laugh she had only ever heard from Haruto when Akari fell off something. "That's a terrible name."

"It's a pencil. Look at it."

He looked at it. "...It is a pencil."

"I know."

The next night, she named a round fuzzy patch of stars the Rice Ball, and didn't explain. He didn't ask her to. The night after that he named one the Commander's Car, and she looked at him sharply, and he only smiled at the sky.

Abriel told her stories, strange and tender ones. Some of them weren't stories. Val didn't know how he knew all of it, or why.

"The Church of the Saints says they fell fighting for us," he said one night, about the saints. "That they went up against Heaven for our sake, and lost, and that's why they're holy. And the people above Umbral have an older book. They call it the Testament. In the Testament, the saints have a different name." He drank from his can. "They call them devils."

"Which is it?"

"Neither." He pointed up, at the Moon. "There are three of them that matter. The one inside the Moon is called Sariel. The one that broke apart into the asteroid belt, the one they've been carving up for exo flesh for fifty years, is Azazel. And under the ice on Europa, there's Semjaza, the biggest, the one they used to open the door in 2044. They weren't martyrs, and they weren't devils. They were rebels. Long before there were any people, they went up to Heaven to ask a question, and when nobody answered, they went to war. And they lost. Heaven threw them down, and their bodies are still lying where they fell."

"What was the question?"

Abriel was quiet for a moment.

"Nobody remembers," he said. "That's the thing about a question nobody answers. After long enough, the silence is all that's left. Everyone remembers that there was no answer. Nobody remembers what they asked."

Val looked down at the can in her hands.

"You know about the children," he said, on another night. It wasn't a question.

"Someone told me," Val said. "The giants. They're the saints' children. They're born in their fathers' graves, out in the cold, and they fall toward the Root, because it's the only piece of their fathers on Earth. They're trying to get home." She had heard it in a voice coming out of Navis's walls, in a corridor turned to frost, and she had never told anyone. She told him. "Every one I ever killed."

"Yes," said Abriel. "Every one." He didn't say it to hurt her. He said it the way you'd say the sea was salt. "Some of them never fall, though. Some of them are born, and they just stay there, in the dark, where they were born. Waiting for someone to tell them where home is."

He talked about the Moon most of all.

He said it had been asleep for a very long time, longer than there had been people to look at it, and that it dreamed. He said it had dreamed of a son. He said the craters were where the dreams had pressed too hard, from the inside, trying to get out. He said that on nights like this one, when the Moon was red, you could almost hear it breathing, and he would go quiet and tilt his head, listening, and Val would listen too and hear nothing but the wind in the ruins.

He talked about the Moon the way people talk about someone they love and have never met. Val listened. It was like listening to a boy talk about his father's grave.

On the fifth night, she asked him.

"What was it like? On the Moon."

"Dark," Abriel said. "Cold. Very quiet. You'd think it would be lonely, but you have to know what people are to be lonely, and I didn't, yet." He turned the can around in his fingers. "The first thing I remember is the dark. I was lying on stone, at the bottom of a crater, inside the ribs of something enormous, in a place that had been made for me before I was there to see it. I didn't know what I was. I didn't have a name. I didn't know there was such a thing as a name."

He was quiet for a while.

"And then a girl came."

Val looked at him.

"On the Moon?"

"On the Moon." He smiled at the sky. "I don't know how. Nobody else ever came. But she was there, kneeling beside me in the dark. She had short hair. She'd been crying for a long time; you could tell, the way you can tell when it's been raining. She took my hand. She was the first thing I ever touched." He opened his hand on his knee and looked at it, as if the touch were still there. "And she asked me for something. Nobody had ever asked me for anything. I didn't know you could."

"What did she ask?"

Abriel turned his head and looked at her, for a long moment, in the red light.

"She asked me to make Valeria happy," he said.

The wind went across the roof. One of the slow lights crossed the sky behind his head and burned out.

"That's me," Val said. It came out barely a whisper. "That's... my name."

"I didn't know that, then. I didn't know who Valeria was. I didn't know what happy was. So I waited until I found out." He looked back up at the Moon, and his voice went soft, almost shy, the way people's voices go when they say something they've practiced alone. "I waited a very long time on the Moon. I'd have waited longer. I think that makes me a fool." He smiled at the sky. "I don't mind."

"Who was she?" said Val.

"I never found out." He smiled. "She looked a little like you."

Val thought he was teasing her. She looked away, at the Pencil, low over the hills, and felt her face go hot under the hood, and was glad of the dark.

He wasn't afraid of her, and he didn't hate her. He didn't flinch when she spoke or watch her like a fire. He asked her questions and waited for the answers, and she found herself giving them.

About the school: Akari's punch, on the third day, square in the mouth, and how Akari had tried to make it even afterward by asking Val to hit her back. Nanami's good mornings, every single day, to someone who never once said it back. Haruto's documentary, which he narrated in a nature-program whisper while following her down the hall, calling her *the subject*. About Alex's omurice, torn down the middle, the ugliest omurice in Hakobune, which she had eaten all of. About the eggs on her tray in Ararat on mission days, rolled soft and sweet the way the nanny used to make them, from a giant with frost on his neck who said he was a jinn with powers. About the other eggs, after he was gone, torn and burnt, from some new cook who didn't know how.

About April. Both of them. The one in the white room who had asked seven questions and then an eighth, and the one in the hangar who didn't ask anything.

Abriel listened to all of it with his whole attention, the way April had listened in the white room, leaning in slightly, as if each answer were a gift being handed to him one at a time.

Lying awake afterward, in the narrow concrete room with the vent breathing on her, she thought he was the only person left in the world who understood her.

On the sixth night, they sat on the edge of the roof in a long, easy silence, and the Moon was very large, and she had her hood down for once because the wind had dropped. He was looking at her instead of at the sky. She noticed and didn't know what to do about it, so she kept looking at the sky.

"You're beautiful this time around," Abriel said.

All Val heard was the word *beautiful*, which she had never heard from anyone.

She didn't hear the rest of the sentence. She didn't think about it until much later, in a place outside time, where she had nothing to do but think.

A week passed. A week of Val quietly watching April from a distance, holding herself back from going to her. A week of star-gazing with Abriel. A week of peace, without exos, without hate, without pain.

She watched the girl with April's face in the hangar every morning, from the catwalk above Unit 00, with her hood up. The girl stood in front of the white unit for hours. She ate when a bell rang, exactly what she was given, at a table by herself, and stopped when the bell rang again. Once Val passed her in a corridor, close enough to touch, and the girl stopped and looked at her and said, "Valeria Kane," as a statement, as a fact, and then nothing.

Val stood there and waited for the question. There was always a question. There had always been a question.

None came. The girl walked on.

It was the loudest silence Val had ever heard.

On the fifth morning she saw Abriel cross the hangar floor below her, on his way somewhere, and stop beside the girl in front of Unit 00.

"Good morning," he said to her, warmly, as if they were old friends.

The girl turned her head and looked at him and said nothing at all. After a moment, Abriel smiled and went on his way. When he came out onto the roof that night, Val asked him about it.

"Why do you say good morning to her? She doesn't say it back."

"She doesn't know what it means," Abriel said. "Nobody's ever taught her." He handed Val her can. "Somebody said it to you every day for months, didn't they? Before you said it back."

"I never said it back."

"Not yet," said Abriel.

On the seventh night, the wind came back, and they sat closer together against it, and Val asked him.

She had seen the ruins from below, from the back of a salvage truck and the floor of a frozen shelter. She had seen the frost and the hanging districts and the burning mountain. But she had never asked anyone what had really happened that night, because no one had needed to tell her; everyone told her anyway, every day, with their faces. And he was the only one who had been there, inside it, with her.

"What happened to the world?" she said. "After you stopped 04?"

Abriel took her hand.

He didn't answer at once. He looked down at her hand in his, and then up at her face, and something passed over his own face that she didn't recognize then and would recognize later: the look of someone at the top of a staircase they have climbed many times before.

"You can't go back," he said. "Are you sure you want to know?"

"Yes, please."

**Arc 53: You Did This**

He led her up through the frozen ruins of headquarters, up a stairwell that went on long after the walls around it had fallen away, past floors that were only floors now, open to the wind.

It wasn't the stairwell to the roof. It was another one, on the far side of the ruins, in what had been the east tower, the tallest thing in Hakobune once, where the Commander's office had looked out over the harbor. The tower had broken in half during the Almost. The bottom half still stood, and the stairs went up through it, switchback after switchback, concrete slabs bolted to a steel spine, with nothing on either side but air and the long fall and the frost.

Abriel went first. He held her hand and climbed half a step ahead, and every few flights he looked back at her, to see that she was still there.

Halfway up, Val knew.

She knew the stairs. She knew the cold on her face, and the way the wind came across the open floors in long shoves that tried to push you sideways. She knew the steel railing, iced over, and the way your glove stuck to it if you held on too long. She knew the way he kept half a step ahead of her and kept looking back. She knew his hand.

She had been here before. She had been here months ago, lying on a hangar floor under headquarters with the sprinklers raining down on her, looking out through someone else's eyes. Climbing these stairs. Holding this hand. Looking back, every few flights, at a girl in a hood.

Her hood was up against the wind, the way it was everywhere she went now, and her stomach turned over, because she finally understood whose face she had never gotten a clean look at.

It had been her own.

She stopped on the stairs. Abriel stopped too, a step above her, and looked back, the way he had in the vision, the way he had every few flights. He didn't say anything. He waited.

She could have turned around. She thought about it, there on the stairs, with the wind pushing at her. She could let go of his hand and go back down and never know the rest. Keep the sentence she'd been carrying, the one that ended where the vision ended. *04 did this.* Keep it forever, the way she'd kept luck.

She didn't let go. She went on climbing.

At the top of the broken tower, where the floor simply ended in open air, he stopped.

The whole ruined world spread out beneath them. The hanging districts, turning slowly in the dark with their icicles dripping. The frost, going on to the horizon in every direction, over hills, over farms, over towns she would never know the names of. The seam in the sky, still pulsing, like a wound that wouldn't close. The red Moon. And far off, Fuji, still burning. No sign of life anywhere. Nothing. Just the cold ice.

It was the last frame of the vision. Exactly. Every detail. She had seen this view through his eyes so many times that standing in it now felt like stepping into a photograph of herself.

"This is what happened," Abriel said gently. "04 did this."

He turned to her.

Val held her breath on the half-second where the vision had cut out, the one she had been living inside since she woke. She heard her own heartbeat. She heard the wind. She heard the faint, slow drip of a hanging district a kilometer away. She stood in that half-second for what felt like a very long time, the way you stand at the edge of a step in the dark, with your foot out, feeling for the floor.

"And you did this, Val."

It wasn't an accusation. It was a fact, laid down between them like a stone.

Somewhere under it, she had always known. She had known on the shelter steps, saying *04 did this* over and over until it was only noise. She had known in the bay with Seus leaning over her. She had known in a hollow's capsule, feeling nothing, choosing nothing, so that nothing would be hers. She had built her whole life since waking on half a sentence, and she had known the whole time that it was half.

There was nothing left to hold on to. Not luck. Not 04. Not her father.

Val Kane sank down at the edge of the broken tower with the whole ruined world in front of her, and she broke.

She cried the way she hadn't cried at nine, or in the port, or anywhere in between: all of it at once, until she couldn't breathe. She cried for Seus, crushed in her hands. For Akari and Haruto and Nanami, and an empty shelter, and a pencil in a frozen desk. For Dasha, mid-sentence, and Tom under the door, and Sae in the pass, and Gage in the street, and Kuze in the sky, finding her name. For April, holding her in the white room. For the April who stood in front of a machine and didn't ask anything. She puked, she choked, she coughed, she wanted to jump. She was too afraid to.

Abriel sat down beside her and held her, and he didn't tell her it was all right, because it wasn't.

He held her while she shook. He held her while the wind came across the top of the tower and tried to push them both off it. He held her while the falling lights crossed the sky one by one and burned out, and the Moon moved, and the cold came up through the concrete into their legs. He didn't say anything at all. He just stayed.

When she could hear again, he was still holding her hand. Her face was stiff with frozen tears.

"You're going to ask me to fix it," Abriel said.

She hadn't said anything. She couldn't have. But it was already there in her, the shape of it, rising up out of the wreck of her like the only thing left standing, and he looked at it the way he looked at everything, as if he had seen it before.

"Everyone thinks it can be undone." He looked up at the seam. "The door's still open. Heaven is outside time. Every day that ever was is still happening up there, somewhere. So it must be possible to go back to one of them and change it." He was quiet for a moment. "I've brought you up these stairs more times than there are stars, Val. Every time, you ask me to fix it. Every time, I say yes. And every time, we end up right back here, on these stairs, looking at this."

She stared at him.

"So this time I'm saying no," Abriel said gently. "You can't undo it. I've tried."

She didn't understand half of what he'd said. She understood the no. It went into her the way the cold did, all the way down, and found nothing there to stop it.

"How many times?" she whispered.

"I stopped counting." He turned her hand over in his and looked at it, the way he had looked at his own hand on the roof. "You'd think it would be sad. It isn't. Every time, I get the roof, and the cans, and the stars. Every time, I get to name them again." He smiled at her. "Every time, I get you. It's the only life I've got. I don't mind it."

"Then what's the point?" Her voice cracked. "If it can't be undone. If nothing up there can be changed. What's the point of any of it?"

Abriel was quiet for a long time. A slow light crossed the sky above the seam and went out.

"There's one thing up there you haven't done," he said.

She looked at him.

"In the white room," Abriel said, "she asked you one more question. And you didn't answer it."

Val stopped breathing.

"I was there. When I put the Sword through, I felt her ask." He looked up at the long bright line in the sky. "Everything that ever happened is still happening up there. Which means she's still asking. She's been asking for seven years, in a white room that's going gray, waiting for you to say something." He squeezed her hand. "You can't change what happened, Val. But you could still answer her."

"Through the door," Val said.

"Through the door." He didn't look away from it. "It's hungry. It eats wanting. Every time, you go up there to take something back, and it takes everything instead." He turned and looked at her. "So don't go up there to take. Go up there to give her something."

"And if I can't? If I want too much?"

"Then I'll be there," said Abriel. "I'm always there."

Val looked at the seam in the sky for a long time. She looked at it the way she had once looked at a girl in a white room: with everything in her.
